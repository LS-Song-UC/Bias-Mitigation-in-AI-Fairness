## 1. "Positive contribution" presumes a direction the framework withheld — `[resolved]`

> **Resolved by the author's amendment** in
> [`where-we-come-from-where-were-going.md`](where-we-come-from-where-were-going.md).
>
> This idea is the **treatment** stage and presupposes a completed diagnosis.
> The sign is not assumed here — it is *inherited* from ideas 1–4, which have
> already determined that a harm exists and where it sits. "Positive
> contribution" means movement away from a diagnosed harm, and is
> sign-determinate for that reason.
>
> The amendment also removes identification accuracy from the objective, so the
> idea no longer competes with idea 4 over the same quantity. Attribution is
> what gets adjusted; identification stays upstream.
>
> This closes the internal-contradiction risk. What remains is a **sequencing
> dependency**, not an objection: the treatment framework is only as sound as
> the diagnosis feeding it, so the recurring burden-of-proof question stays live
> upstream in ideas 1–4. It does not need re-answering here.

### Original statement

The sharpest one, because it is internal rather than external.

Ideas 1–4 are built on refusing to assume that a deviation is harmful:
bias is neutral until shown otherwise, harm sits in the inference, a gap is
diagnostic rather than a defect, a hypothesis is licensed until defeated. The
whole apparatus exists to withhold judgement on the *sign* of a disparity until
it has been investigated.

"Shifting in the direction of positive contribution" reintroduces exactly that
sign as given. If you do not yet know whether a gap is licensed, you do not know
whether closing it is an improvement — and a framework that measures adjustments
by how far they move in a presumed-good direction has quietly readmitted the
assumption the first four ideas spent their effort removing.

**What it would take to close.** Either derive the direction from the earlier
machinery — positive means *removal of an unlicensed inference*, which is
sign-determinate under ideas 2 and 4 — or state that this idea presupposes a
completed harm determination and only applies downstream of it.

*Outcome: the author took the second route, and the first turns out to be
compatible with it rather than an alternative — the diagnosis supplies the sign,
the treatment stage consumes it.*

## 2. Relative framing cuts both ways — `[live]`

A 500% increase on a 1% baseline is the strongest version of the argument. The
same arithmetic makes trivial absolute changes look transformative, which is the
standard criticism of advocacy statistics — and a reviewer who does this for a
living will apply it immediately.

Adopting relative framing means also accepting the cases where it flatters: a
0.1% → 0.6% move is also 500%, and may affect almost nobody.

**What it would take to close.** The epidemiological answer, which is settled and
cheap to adopt: report both, plus the absolute number of people affected. That
converts a contested rhetorical move into standard methodology, and the
number-needed-to-treat analogue does the reconciling work. Adopting an existing
convention here is stronger than defending a novel one.

## 3. Small denominators carry large uncertainty — `[live]`

The regime that makes the ratio argument compelling is exactly the regime where
the ratio is least stable. At a 1% base rate, subgroup counts are small,
confidence intervals on the ratio are wide and asymmetric, and a 500% headline
may be substantially noise.

Without uncertainty reported alongside, the metric most sensitive to the cases
of interest is also the most likely to manufacture findings — and the direction
of that error favours whoever is reporting.

**What it would take to close.** Interval estimates on ratio metrics as a
requirement rather than an option, and an explicit minimum-count regime below
which ratios are not reported as headline results. Mechanical, but it needs to
be in the protocol rather than left to the analyst.

## 4. Predicted social impact is the weakest cell in the 2×2 — `[live]`

Three distinct problems, worth separating:

- **Feasibility.** Ex ante prediction of social impact has a poor track record;
  effects that matter often appear only at scale, in interaction with other
  systems, and over time horizons longer than any assessment.
- **Conflict of interest.** A developer assessing the predicted impact of their
  own system is not a neutral instrument, and impact assessment regimes
  generally have to solve this institutionally rather than methodologically.
- **Goodhart.** Once a predicted-impact score becomes a target, it stops
  measuring what it measured.

The fragment's "when possible" already concedes some of this. The risk is that a
protocol listing four cells implies all four are equally attainable, when one is
qualitatively harder than the others.

**What it would take to close.** Mark the cell's epistemic status explicitly in
the protocol, and say what the framework does when prediction is unavailable —
which will be the common case. A protocol that degrades gracefully is more
credible than one that assumes its hardest input.

## 5. Terminology — `[live]`, trivial

"Post-vivo" is not standard. The established pairs are *ex ante / ex post* or
*prospective / retrospective*. Worth fixing before anything is drafted, since
the coinage will read as unfamiliarity with the assessment literature the idea
is otherwise engaging with.
