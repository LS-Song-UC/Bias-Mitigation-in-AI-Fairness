# Proportional Representation

**Status:** `[thinking]` — posed as an open question, not an assertion. Kept
that way deliberately; the answer may be the contribution.

---

## The idea (as captured)

Should 10% of the demographic be given 10% consideration?

Established frameworks in many legal processes exemplify the widely accepted
consensus that a person is innately treated equal in representation
power/weight to its fellow. Unless otherwise diminished due to extenuating
circumstances (criminal acts, indictments), a person holds legal power by right
of legitimate residency within its bounding legal system, and that power is
distributed equally amongst all participants of the system.

Does this concept apply equally in societal representation of special groups
within a demographic? Should 10% population within a demographic be given its
exact due 10% of representation/consideration?

---

## The question, sharpened

Is proportionality *owed* — a baseline entitlement that any deviation must
justify — or is it merely one possible outcome, correct when it happens to
follow from something else?

The legal analogy supplies a strong prior for the first reading: equal
individual weight, aggregated, yields proportional group weight automatically.
Whether that transfers to representation inside a model is the open question.

## Why it might matter

It is the natural target metric. "Match the population distribution" is
measurable, auditable, and intuitively fair, which is exactly why it is so
widely used — and why, if the answer is *no*, that needs arguing rather than
asserting.

This also has a direct formal counterpart: **demographic parity** (statistical
parity) is precisely "10% of the population gets 10% of the outcomes." The
question is therefore not new as mathematics, but the legal-legitimacy framing
of it may be. Worth establishing early whether the contribution is the criterion
or the justification for it.

## Relationship to ideas 1 and 2

This is where the three may lock together — or collide.

Ideas 1 and 2 argue: do not strip group-conditional structure indiscriminately;
audit the *inference* instead. Enforced proportionality is arguably a form of
indiscriminate mitigation — it imposes a distribution regardless of whether the
deviation came from an unlicensed inference or from legitimate structure.

That suggests an answer to the question this idea poses:

> Proportionality is not owed as such. What is owed is the absence of
> unlicensed inference. Where a 10% group is 10% of the relevant population on
> licensed grounds, proportionality follows as a *consequence*. Where it does
> not follow, the question is whether the gap traces to a licensed pathway or an
> unlicensed one — and only the latter is a harm to be corrected.

If that holds, the three ideas form a spine: neutral definition → harm located
at inference → proportionality as diagnostic rather than target. A gap from
proportionality becomes a *signal to investigate*, not a defect to erase.

This is a candidate synthesis, not a settled one. It should be argued rather
than assumed, and it may be wrong — see the objections file, where the strongest
challenge is that it makes disparity very easy to rationalise.

## A distinction that probably matters

"Representation/consideration" may be three different things, with three
different answers:

| Layer | What proportionality would mean | Plausible answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Data** | Sample the group at its population rate | Possibly *too little* — equal subgroup error may require oversampling |
| **Consideration** | Weight it proportionally in the objective | Depends entirely on the objective |
| **Outcome** | Allocate results at the population rate | The contested one; this is demographic parity |

The legal analogy is strongest for the third and weakest for the first. Worth
deciding which layer the research is actually about before answering.

## Open questions

- Which layer is the research about: data, consideration, or outcome?
- Proportional to *what* reference population? Overall population, applicant
  pool, qualified pool, and affected population give different answers, and the
  choice is doing most of the work.
- If proportionality is diagnostic rather than owed, what threshold of deviation
  triggers investigation?
