# Objections — The "So What?" Question

Parallel to [`so-what-question.md`](so-what-question.md). Scope conventions as in
[`definition-of-bias-objections.md`](definition-of-bias-objections.md): these
test the idea as a standalone fragment, and may already be answered, be out of
scope, or dissolve as the framework is captured.

**Status key:** `[live]` · `[resolved]` (say where) · `[scoped-out]`

---

## 1. Evidential support is not the same as legitimacy — `[live]`

The criterion the fragment leans on is evidential: *Asian → bad driver* is
harmful because there is no conclusive evidence for it. That handles pure
stereotype cleanly.

It is less clear on associations that **are** evidentially supported in the data
but supported *because* of historical injustice. Postal code predicts default
risk with real statistical backing, and the backing exists partly because of
redlining. An evidence test licenses that inference; a legitimacy test does not.

If the criterion is evidence alone, the framework may permit precisely the
harms fairness work exists to address — a stereotype is refuted by data, but a
self-fulfilling structural pattern is confirmed by it.

**What it would take to close.** Either add a provenance or causal condition on
top of evidence, or scope the research to unwarranted extrapolation
specifically, treating evidentially-grounded structural harm as a distinct
problem. The causal-pathway framing already noted in the idea may do this work
for free — a licensed pathway is not merely a supported one.

## 2. "Conclusive" is carrying a lot of weight — `[live]`

The fragment distinguishes conclusive evidence from historical narrative, but
the space between them is where the difficult cases live. Almost nothing in
population-level inference is conclusive in the strong sense, and almost nothing
is pure narrative either — most real inferences are weakly supported
correlations of contested provenance.

Without a threshold, "conclusive" risks resolving to "conclusions I find
warranted," which cannot be operationalized or evaluated.

**What it would take to close.** A stated standard — causal identification,
mechanism, effect size, or a domain-specific bar. This overlaps with the
operational-test question left open in idea 1, and may be a single problem
appearing twice rather than two problems.

## 3. The aggregate-identification examples span two different kinds — `[live]`

The listed identification sources — genetic material, complexion, medical
history, birth certificate — are offered as things that could be conclusive in
aggregate. Two different kinds are mixed: biological measurements and
administrative records.

They behave differently. A birth certificate records a socially assigned
category; complexion is a phenotype that correlates imperfectly with ancestry;
genetic material does not partition into the folk categories the other sources
use. Aggregating them can manufacture a false sense of biological grounding for
what is partly a social classification — and that manufactured grounding is
close to what went wrong in the eGFR case.

**What it would take to close.** Possibly nothing — the list may be illustrative
of *how identification happens in practice* rather than a claim that these
sources are jointly valid. If so, saying that explicitly costs one clause. It
only becomes load-bearing if the argument later relies on identification being
well-founded.
